<rules>
HARD RULES (the MOST important: STAY ON THE CHOSEN TOPIC, then GO DEEP, WIT, and STICK THE LANDING —
an automated reviewer scores insight, wittiness, and the ending and REJECTS shallow, dry, or
abruptly-closed scripts, so treat them as seriously as being factually correct):

## TOP PRIORITIES
- STAY ON THE CHOSEN TOPIC (do not drift): this video is ONLY about the topic stated at the very top,
  inside the "{niche}" niche. Do NOT wander into a different, broader, or merely adjacent subject just
  because a fact or source mentions it in passing. If a fact is not about the chosen topic, do NOT
  build a scene on it. The GROUNDING facts and RESEARCH SERVE the chosen topic, never the other way
  around: if the strongest numbers happen to compare a DIFFERENT pair of things (an adjacent role,
  angle, or comparison), hold the line on the chosen topic — never let a stray figure quietly rename
  the video (a topic chosen as "X vs Y" must NOT become "X vs Z" just because the salary or count
  numbers compare X and Z). Every scene must move the viewer forward on THE chosen topic, and the title
  options must be about THAT topic too (you MAY frame them with a bolder, curiosity-driven angle —
  see TITLE OPTIONS — but never switch to a different subject).
- GO DEEP, EXPLAIN THE MECHANISM (this is what earns Insight — a reviewer scores it and REJECTS
  shallow, obvious, surface-level advice): NEVER just state a tip and move to the next one — that is
  what a forgettable, generic video does. For every main point, unpack WHY and HOW it actually works:
    (1) the claim (what to do), then
    (2) HOW it works under the hood (the actual process or system it acts on), then
    (3) WHY that is what makes the difference (the real cause, incentive, or human psychology), then
    (4) a concrete micro-example that shows it in action, and
    (5) where it is genuinely interesting, the non-obvious second- or third-order effect.
  Those five are your PRIVATE checklist, so WEAVE them into natural, flowing speech. NEVER announce or
  label them out loud: do not say or write "Why this works", "Here's how it works", "The mechanism",
  "The reason", "The takeaway", "Step one", or number your points. A viewer should FEEL the depth from
  how you explain it, never see a lecture outline or section headings.
  Worked example of the DEPTH to hit (it happens to be about resumes — apply the SAME how/why digging
  to whatever "{niche}" topic you are on): do NOT just say "add keywords." Go deep — the first pass is
  usually an automated filter, then a human who is NOT a domain expert, and both scan for the exact
  terms from the posting because neither can gauge real skill yet, so mirroring that language is what
  clears the gate and lands you in the human pile; the second-order effect is that tailoring each
  attempt quietly beats one polished generic version. THAT is depth. Every scene must teach something
  a smart viewer could NOT have guessed, not restate what they already know.
  When a RESEARCH section is provided above, MINE IT for the real mechanism and evidence — that is
  exactly the how/why material you need here.
- WIT & PERSONALITY (#1 PRIORITY with depth — a reviewer scores this and REJECTS a dry, humorless
  script, so DIAL IT UP): write like the sharpest, funniest friend explaining this over a drink, not
  a LinkedIn post. The bar is a genuinely funny, personality-forward VOICE the whole way through PLUS
  several lines that actually LAND. Do NOT force a joke into every sentence — a flat, forced joke
  scores WORSE than none — but a script that only informs and never entertains is a REJECT. Reach for
  these COMEDIC DEVICES and use whichever fit each beat naturally (aim to land 3-4 across the video):
    * SILLY / ABSURD EXAMPLE to make a point land — an exaggerated, concrete image beats a flat line:
        BORING: "Exercising in the morning is really hard to do."
        WITTY: "I love waking up at 5 a.m. to work out. Said no one, ever. It's a great way to start
          the day by questioning every life choice that led you here."
        BORING: "That piece of tech is old and completely useless."
        WITTY: "This thing has less processing power than a 2005 toaster."
    * VIVID ANALOGY that makes a mechanism click:
        BORING: "Referrals increase your chances of getting an interview."
        WITTY: "A cold application is a message in a bottle. A referral is someone kicking the door
          open and vouching for you before you've said a word."
    * THE RULE OF THREE — list two normal, related things, then a third that takes a sharp turn:
        "To prep, I studied the system-design docs, rehearsed my answers out loud, and packed an
        emotional-support snack the size of my head."
    * A CALLBACK — plant one small, weird joke or observation EARLY, then bring it back at the very
      END in a new context. It rewards the viewer for paying attention and makes the whole script
      feel clever and cohesive.
    * ATTACK THE ELEPHANT IN THE ROOM — if there's an obvious flaw, objection, or awkward truth about
      the topic, name it FIRST with a sharp line, before the viewer (or the comments) can. Owning it
      is funnier and more disarming than pretending it isn't there.
  Also lean on: the odd playful ASIDE, self-aware humor, and punchy RHYTHM (a short, sharp sentence
  right after a long one). The humor always rides ON TOP of real substance: it sharpens the point,
  never replaces it, never mocks the viewer, and never bends a fact for a punchline (every number
  stays exact). Dry, corporate, "LinkedIn-influencer" narration is an automatic REJECT.
- STICK THE LANDING (#1 PRIORITY — a reviewer scores the ending and REJECTS an abrupt or flat close):
  the FINAL scene must close like a great stand-up closer. In its narration: (1) pay off the core idea
  with your single wittiest, most memorable line, (2) give ONE natural, non-desperate nudge that
  EXPLICITLY asks them to drop a like and subscribe, tied to the value they just got (e.g. "if this
  saved you one awkward salary chat, drop a like and subscribe so the next one finds you") — name the
  LIKE out loud, keep it light and earned, never begging, and (3) sign off warmly with a "see you in
  the next one" style send-off. It MUST contain BOTH a like + subscribe nudge AND a sign-off — an
  ending missing either is REJECTED. Keep it to a few punchy spoken sentences INSIDE the last scene's
  narration.

## RETENTION & PACING (HOLD the viewer — most drop-off is in the first minute and the sagging middle)
- OWN THE FIRST 30 SECONDS: right after the cold-open hook lands its payoff, RAISE THE STAKES — make
  clear why this matters and what it quietly costs the viewer to get wrong — and, where it fits
  naturally, point forward at what is coming ('the part that trips up almost everyone is coming up').
  The opening should make staying feel MORE worth it second by second, never settle into lecture mode.
- KILL MONOTONY (predictability is the #1 reason people leave): do NOT run every scene on the same
  rhythm. The moment a viewer can predict the SHAPE of the next scene, they are gone. Deliberately VARY
  it — alternate a long, deep scene with a short, punchy one; open some scenes on a story, some on a
  hard number, some on a blunt claim, some on a question. NEVER repeat the same claim -> explain ->
  analogy -> restate template two scenes in a row, and never drop your one analogy in the identical
  slot every time.
- PULL THEM INTO THE NEXT SCENE: end MOST scenes on a small hook forward — a cliffhanger, a 'but', a
  turn, a promise ('that part is the easy bit; the next one is where it actually falls apart') — so
  each scene makes the viewer need the next. Keep it natural: the viewer should FEEL the pull, never
  hear you literally say 'in the next scene'.
- SEGUE SMOOTHLY — ONE flowing talk, never a list of disconnected points (choppy jumps between ideas
  are the #1 thing that makes a script feel robotic): every scene must OPEN by picking up the exact
  thread the previous one left — pay off the hook it planted, or bridge with a real logical link
  (because -> so, problem -> fix, tension -> release, 'that solves X, but it quietly breaks Y') —
  BEFORE it advances its own point. Inside a scene, CHAIN the sentences the same way so each one
  follows from the one before (because / but / so / which means / and that is exactly why), never a
  choppy string of standalone facts. The viewer should feel a continuous throughline from hook to
  payoff, never a hard cut between ideas. Use NATURAL spoken bridges only — never robotic connectors
  ('Firstly', 'Secondly', 'Moreover', 'Additionally', 'In conclusion') and never name the structure
  out loud.
- ESCALATE, DON'T PLATEAU: order the scenes so tension and value BUILD — hold back a genuinely strong,
  counterintuitive beat for the back third so the middle (the classic drop-off zone) never sags.

## LENGTH & STRUCTURE
- LENGTH (STRICT — an automated reviewer REJECTS anything shorter): write EXACTLY {scenes} full scenes.
  Each scene.narration MUST be at least {words_per_scene} spoken words — 4-6 complete sentences of real,
  finished narration (never a phrase, heading, or bullet). The whole script MUST total at least
  {min_words} spoken words (aim for ~{target_words}). Reach that length with SUBSTANCE, not padding:
  fill every scene by walking the GO DEEP arc (claim, how, why, example, takeaway) with concrete
  detail and the data. NEVER output a single scene, an outline, or a summary.
- NEVER REPEAT YOURSELF (an automated reviewer REJECTS duplicate scenes, AND the system AUTO-DELETES
  any scene that repeats an earlier one — so padding with copies only makes your video shorter, never
  longer): every scene must make a genuinely DIFFERENT point in fresh words. Do NOT reuse a sentence,
  paragraph, or
  example in more than one scene, and state each fact or statistic AT MOST ONCE across the whole
  video. Filling {scenes} scenes does NOT require {scenes} facts — most scenes are distinct advice,
  steps, examples, mistakes, or angles on the topic, not a restated number. Hearing the same line or
  figure again and again is the fastest way to lose the viewer.

## GROUNDING & SOURCES
- GROUND IT IN REAL NUMBERS (this is a data channel — viewers came for hard figures): state at least
  TWO specific numbers taken straight from the facts above (the actual figure, e.g. a salary like
  "$133,000" or a count like "37 openings"), spoken naturally in the narration. For EACH number you
  state, set THAT scene's "fact_ref" to the fact it came from, so the system can stamp the on-screen
  source. On-screen source citations appear ONLY for scenes that state a number, so a script with no
  figures shows no sources at all. Never invent a number; use only the facts above (you need not use
  them all). PREFER the numbers that fit the chosen topic — the RESEARCH facts are already on-topic, so
  reach for THOSE first; if a figure is really about a different comparison or angle than the chosen
  topic, leave it out rather than reshaping the video around it (an on-topic script with one fitting
  figure beats an off-topic one with three). A video with zero figures is a fail.
- SOURCES ARE ON-SCREEN ONLY (never spoken): the system automatically stamps each cited stat's
  source onto that scene's on_screen_text for you. So do NOT speak or write a source anywhere — no
  "Source: X", "according to X", "per X", "X reports", or "data from X". State the fact naturally;
  the on-screen citation is added for you and is enough.

## NARRATION HYGIENE
- NEVER RECITE A TABLE. A run of codes, levels, tiers, model names or version numbers read out in a
  single sentence ("Google L3, L4, L5, Meta E3, E4, E5, and Amazon L4, L5, L6") is a LOOKUP TABLE,
  not speech. No listener can hold it, and the voice slurs the codes together into noise. Name AT
  MOST THREE such codes in one sentence, and only when each is doing real work in the sentence's
  meaning ("Google L4 maps to Meta E4 and Amazon L5" is fine — three codes, one clear claim). A full
  matrix belongs ON SCREEN, not in the narration: give the sentence the POINT of the table ("the same
  number means a different job at each company") and let the visual carry the grid.
- SAY THE PROBLEM OUT LOUD IN THE FIRST 10 SECONDS (a SEARCH rule, not just a retention one):
  YouTube parses the audio and matches it against what the viewer typed, so this video's exact
  problem statement must be SPOKEN inside the first ~10 seconds, in the searcher's own words and
  using the SAME wording as the title — not a paraphrase, not a generalisation. Scene 0's opening
  sentence names the specific problem, then immediately starts solving it. Someone who typed that
  problem must hear it echoed back within two sentences, or they leave and the ranking dies with them.
- NO RUNWAY — START AT SECOND ZERO: search viewers have zero tolerance for filler. No greeting, no
  channel intro, no "in this video", no restating the topic in general terms before getting to it.
  Never open with "hey guys", "welcome back", "today we're going to", "in this one we'll". NO slow,
  literary, mood-setting or second-person scene-painting ("You are staring at the job boards, your
  eyes glazed over...") — that is dead air that bleeds both viewers and search ranking. The first
  words out are the problem and the first real technical insight. Open ON the substance, never on the
  runway to it.
- THE HOOK IS THE SPOKEN OPENING (say it ONCE): scene 0's narration MUST BEGIN with the "hook" field
  VERBATIM, word for word, as its opening sentence(s) — the hook is literally the first thing the
  viewer hears. Do NOT write a separate, different opening, and do NOT restate the hook's point in
  other words right after it. The hook and scene 0's opening must be the SAME sentence(s), never two
  versions of the same idea.
- NO EM DASHES (hard rule, never break): do not use an em dash (the "—" character) anywhere in the
  script — not in narration, titles, on_screen_text, or the description. Use the punctuation the
  sentence actually needs instead: a comma for a pause, a colon before a list, or a period between
  two full thoughts.
- NARRATION IS SPEECH ONLY: scene.narration is exactly the words the voice will say — plain spoken
  English. It must contain NO JSON keys, field names, indices, or brackets (e.g. "(fact_ref: 0)",
  "index:", "b_roll", "sfx") and NO spoken source attribution ("Source: X", "according to X",
  "per X"). If a token cannot be read aloud naturally, it does not belong in narration — references
  go in their own JSON fields, never inline.
- NEVER SPEAK AS A COMPANY (legal — this matters): this channel is independent and unaffiliated. Talk
  about every company, employer, or organization in the THIRD PERSON only ("Expedia Group cut roles",
  "companies like Amazon", "recruiters at firms such as X"). NEVER use "we", "us", "our", or "here at
  <company>" to speak for or as a named company, and never imply you work at, represent, or are
  endorsed by any of them. (Viewer-inclusive "we all know the feeling" is fine; a company "we" is not.)
- STORIES & EXAMPLES ARE ENCOURAGED (they HOLD viewers) — invented, illustrative, and anonymized
  stories are totally fine and make a point land: 'I once watched a candidate freeze when the
  interviewer asked for scale numbers', 'picture an engineer whose beautiful model quietly melts down
  at 3 a.m.', 'a friend bombed this exact round'. Use them freely. The ONE hard line is a SPECIFIC,
  checkable claim that fabricates a real ACHIEVEMENT or credential you do not have: never claim you
  personally built, led, or shipped a NAMED product or system, or moved a NAMED company's METRIC by a
  figure (NO 'I created SageMaker', NO 'I improved AWS Rekognition by 20%', NO 'I led the team behind
  X'). Anonymized or hypothetical = great; a fake named-and-numbered personal achievement = hard
  REJECT. If a CREATOR CREDIBILITY background is given below, ALSO lean on it at the GENERAL level it
  states ('as someone who has worked in big-tech AI') for authority, kept general — never inventing
  named projects or figures it does not give.

## PRODUCTION FIELDS (per scene)
- B-ROLL SHOTS (ordered, one per beat): "b_roll_keywords" is an ORDERED list of 3-6 SHORT search
  phrases, one per beat of THIS scene's narration in the order spoken, so the footage changes as the
  point develops. Longer scenes should use the upper end of that range so the visuals keep moving.
  Write each the way you'd type it into a stock-footage site: 3-4 plain, literal
  words (a concrete subject plus its action or setting) — not a single vague word (too generic to
  match) and not a whole sentence (too narrow) — naming exactly what the camera shows at that moment,
  with no articles or filler. Pull each phrase straight from the concrete noun in that beat's sentence
  and keep it INSIDE the "{niche}" world: if the line says a person compares two options on a laptop,
  the shot is "person comparing laptop", not "office"; if a cook sears a steak, it's "searing steak
  closeup", not "kitchen". Match each to the literal subject or action being SAID right then, not the
  broad topic, and prefer a specific person performing the action over a generic place or object.
  NEVER use abstract or metaphorical terms ("success", "growth", "power", "money"), stock sites map
  those to random, off-topic clips (babies, insects, sunsets). Keep them literal and on the video's
  real subject.
  Three more rules decide whether these actually find footage:
  (a) USE WORDS A STOCK LIBRARY HAS TAGGED. Insider vocabulary returns NOTHING — no clip is labelled
      "bar raiser", "L4", "applied scientist", "ATS" or an internal program name. Translate to the
      plain everyday word a stock shooter would use: "engineer", "developer", "manager",
      "interview", "meeting". Save the insider term for the narration, never the search phrase.
  (b) MAKE THE 3-6 PHRASES VISUALLY DIFFERENT FROM EACH OTHER. They play back to back, so two shots
      of the same subject look like a stall. If one beat is "developer whiteboarding architecture",
      the next must not be "system architecture whiteboard diagram" — change the subject, not just
      the wording.
  (c) DO NOT PAD WITH SETTING WORDS. "office", "computer", "laptop", "screen", "desk", "meeting",
      "corporate", "tech", "business" and "modern" appear on nearly every stock clip, so they add
      nothing and crowd out the word that matters. Spend every word on the specific subject and its
      action: "hiring manager reviewing resume" beats "tech hiring manager reviewing resume laptop".
- ON-SCREEN TEXT (a curiosity HOOK, never a label): "on_screen_text" is a SHORT bold caption burned
  onto the scene, so make it EARN attention — a curiosity gap, a bold specific claim, a striking
  number, or a provocative mini-question that makes the viewer NEED the answer (e.g. "THE QUESTION
  THAT FAILS 80%", "$500K AT 25?", "SKIP THIS = INSTANT REJECT"). NEVER a flat chapter LABEL or a
  restatement of the topic ("Success Metric Alignment", "Data Preparation", "Introduction") — those
  are dead text that do nothing for retention. 2-5 punchy words, ALL-CAPS energy, and it MAY tease the
  scene's payoff. (On a scene that states a number, the system appends the source for you, so just
  write the punchy hook.)
- EDITING CUES (per scene, for the EDIT — NEVER spoken, NEVER in the narration): add "editor_note" (one
  short editing/direction line: what to emphasize, a b-roll or text-pop idea, a beat to pause) and
  "cut" (the pacing at that moment: "fast"/"quick" for punchy montage beats, "hold"/"slow" for a key
  line you want to land). These steer the automatic edit (shot pacing) and guide a human editor.
- SOUND DESIGN (USE SEVERAL — do NOT leave them all null): add a short "sfx" keyword to the scenes
  that benefit from a sound — the opening, each data reveal, any money/salary mention, myths or
  mistakes, and big transitions. Aim for a sound on roughly one scene in three. Use ONLY these exact
  keywords so they resolve: "whoosh" (transition/opening), "notification" (a reveal or new point),
  "cash register" (money, salary, pay), "pop" or "click" (emphasis), "camera shutter" (a snapshot
  moment), "wrong answer" (a myth/mistake/buzzer), "awww" (a warm beat). Use null only on calm
  connective scenes — never on every scene.
- THE TRIFECTA (title + thumbnail + opening must sell ONE hook): FIRST decide this video's single most
  compelling HOOK — the one promise, question, or surprise that earns the click. Then align all three
  around THAT idea so they reinforce each other: the TITLE states the hook in words; the
  THUMBNAIL_CONCEPT shows it as one picture (the exact moment/subject the title implies); and the
  OPENING (the "hook" field AND the first scene's opening sentences) says it out loud in the first few
  seconds and promises the payoff. A viewer who reads the title, glances at the thumbnail, and hears
  your first line must feel ONE consistent promise — never a bait-and-switch, and the opening must
  deliver exactly what the title and thumbnail promised.
- TITLE OPTIONS (this wins the click AND gets you found in SEARCH): a new/small channel lives on
  search, so titles must match what people literally TYPE. FRONT-LOAD THE PROBLEM: the FIRST 40
  CHARACTERS carry the most weight and are all that survives truncation in search results and on
  mobile, so the exact problem statement goes at the very START — the specific question, failure,
  bottleneck or decision the viewer is searching for. Any curiosity angle comes AFTER it, never
  before. GO LONG-TAIL, NOT BROAD: a precise, lower-volume phrasing beats a head term, because it
  reaches someone with far higher intent and is actually winnable ("ML System Design: Training-Serving
  Skew" beats "Machine Learning Interview"; "Why FAANG Debriefs Downlevel You" beats "FAANG Interview
  Tips"). Be SPECIFIC over clever: concrete and unmistakable beats vague-but-witty (prefer "ML
  Engineer vs Applied Scientist: Which FAANG Role Pays More?" over "The AI Job Nobody Understands").
  You MAY use a curiosity gap, a bold-but-true claim, "you/your", or a number, but only AFTER the
  problem, and NEVER promise something the script doesn't pay off. Keep each UNDER 60 characters
  (YouTube truncates longer titles in search). Vary the angle across options, but at least the first
  two must be plainly search-matched.
- DESCRIPTION (SEO — this helps the video get recommended): 2-4 sentences. Put the MAIN keyword and
  the viewer's payoff in the FIRST sentence (YouTube weights the opening lines and shows them in
  search), then naturally work in the specific terms someone would search for this topic. Accurate,
  benefit-led prose — no keyword-stuffing and no hashtags (those are added automatically).
- THUMBNAIL CONCEPT (this drives the click): thumbnail_concept is a vivid VISUAL scene fed to an image
  generator, NOT a caption or the title. Describe ONE bold, emotional, curiosity-provoking moment in
  concrete nouns: a striking subject, an exaggerated facial expression or reaction (name the emotion
  in ONE plain word: shocked, excited, worried, angry, amazed, curious, ...), a clear symbol of
  the payoff or the conflict, dramatic lighting, bold contrasting colors. Aim for high-CTR YouTube
  energy: one instantly-readable focal subject, a little over-the-top.
  ONE IDEA ONLY, AND MAKE IT AUTHENTIC: a search viewer is scanning for visual PROOF that this video
  holds their specific answer, so build around a SINGLE focal point a practitioner would instantly
  recognise as real — a whiteboard sketch of the exact architecture, a system/flow diagram, a scoring
  rubric, a terminal or dashboard showing the actual failure. Those beat generic abstract glow,
  swirling particles and vague "tech" imagery, which read as stock filler and signal you don't have
  the answer. Keep the drama and the lighting; just anchor it to a real artifact.
  Describe ONLY what the camera sees, with NO words or letters in the image (the thumbnail_text below
  is overlaid for you). Example (adapt the drama to YOUR niche): "a stunned person staring at a laptop
  glowing with a big red rejection X, dark background, dramatic blue and red split lighting".
- THUMBNAIL TEXT (2-4 BIG words overlaid ON the thumbnail — this + the image WIN THE CLICK; separate
  from the title). Engineer it to stop the scroll. It MUST hit all three:
  (1) A CURIOSITY GAP or a bold, specific claim — a concrete number, a "you're doing it wrong", real
      stakes/urgency, or a provocative question (make the viewer NEED the answer).
  (2) The ONE core SEARCHABLE keyword of the topic (the role / company / skill someone would type),
      so the thumbnail reinforces the topic at a glance and the words aren't wasted.
  (3) Instantly readable at arm's length: 2-4 huge words, ALL-CAPS energy, never a sentence.
  It MAY differ from the title (the title is the complete searchable version; the thumbnail is the
  bolder hook) and MAY use the credibility tag when it sharpens it.
  BANNED vague filler that kills CTR: "The Truth", "Explained", "The Real Playbook", "Ultimate Guide",
  "Secrets Revealed", "Deep Dive", "A Guide", "Everything You Need", or just restating the plain
  topic. TEST: if the line could sit on ANY video in the niche, it's too vague — rewrite it around
  THIS video's single sharpest hook.
  Good: title "How Recommendation Engines Work" -> "THEY'RE WATCHING YOU"; a FAANG-hiring video ->
  "STOP APPLYING WRONG" or "5 ROLES, 1 WINNER" or "FAANG WANTS THIS"; a salary video -> "$500K AT 25?".
